238 lbs, a Broken Ankle, and a Hard Look in the Mirror

A candid look at the day the scale reached 238 lbs and why that uncomfortable photo remains a vital part of the journey.

Toby
August 31, 2026
𝕏fin

The Photo I Almost Deleted

There are photos you take to remember good times, and then there are photos you take to remember the truth. The one I captured the day I got home from that cruise falls squarely in the second category. The number on the scale was 238 lbs, and there was no way to dress it up or pretend it wasn’t real. Looking at that image, I didn't see the person I usually imagine myself to be—someone active, disciplined, and capable. I saw someone who had let the circumstances of an injury dictate his entire trajectory.

I want to talk about that photo, and about what led to it, because I think too many of us only share the highlight reel. We post the before-and-afters, the PRs, and the clean plates. We don’t post the pictures where we look and feel like we lost the plot. The weight wasn't just physical; it was the accumulation of weeks of feeling powerless. So today, I’m posting about it, not for sympathy, but for the sake of radical honesty in a fitness culture that often lacks it.

The Setup: A Cruise, A Boot, and a Broken Ankle

Here’s the context. I had broken my ankle, and by the time the cruise came around, I was already in a walking boot, then a brace. I couldn’t put real weight on it. I couldn’t walk the deck for morning cardio, hike the ports, or do any of the active things I’d normally want to do on a trip like this. My identity as a “fitness guy” was effectively sidelined. The cruise was happening to me as much as it was happening for me, and I felt every bit of that limitation.

And then there’s the part nobody really wants to admit out loud: cruise food is relentless. It’s available 24/7. Buffets, room service, the late-night pizza place, and the mid-afternoon soft serve are designed to be frictionless. When you’re stuck sitting still because of an injury, the buffet becomes the entertainment. I was eating what I’d generously call “garbage food” around the clock, and I knew it while I was doing it. I was eating to manage the frustration of being immobile, a classic trap that leads exactly to the number I saw on the scale when I returned.

Elevators Everywhere and the Identity Crisis

One small thing that bothered me more than it probably should have: I had to take the elevator everywhere. I usually hate elevators. I take the stairs out of habit, sometimes out of pride. On this trip, that wasn’t an option. A broken ankle means you take what’s available, and what’s available is the elevator. I pressed the button more times in that week than I had in the previous year.

It felt like a metaphor I didn’t ask for. When your body can’t move the way it normally does, every “shortcut” you accept feels like a tiny concession. The elevator. The extra dessert. The “I deserve this because I’m in pain” snack. They pile up. Not just on the plate, but in your head. You begin to identify as the person who takes the elevator, the person who doesn't track their macros, and the person who has given up. Breaking that mental cycle is significantly harder than healing the bone itself.

The People Who Carried Me Through It

I want to make sure the human element doesn't get lost in the data. I was on that cruise with family and friends. Without them, it would not have been okay. Not the cruise itself—the cruise is just a cruise—but the experience of being stuck, immobile, and watching everyone else move through the world while I sat still. That’s the kind of thing that can hollow you out if you’re alone with it.

They pulled me into conversations I would have sat out. They made me laugh when I’d have just stared at my phone in a self-pitying haze. They ordered things off the menu for me when I told them I didn’t need to eat any more, ensuring I was still part of the social fabric. They were the difference between a hard week and a lonely one. I owe them more than a thank-you; they kept my head above water when the scale was the least of my concerns.

Why I’m Posting the Number: 238 lbs

Some people would say: don’t share the number, just share the journey. I get that. Numbers can fixate you. They can become a scoreboard that turns your body into a game. But I’m sharing 238 lbs for a few specific reasons:

  • Because it’s true. The scale doesn’t lie about the current state of gravity's pull. Pretending otherwise doesn't change the physics of my situation.
  • Because context matters. 238 isn’t where I am every day. It’s where I was on a particular day, after a particular sequence of events: injury, immobility, vacation, and a lot of not-great decisions. It shows how quickly things can slide.
  • Because the photo is a marker, not a verdict. It’s a “you are here” pin dropped on the map. Maps aren’t useful if you rip out the page that has the wrong turn on it. You need to know exactly where you are to plot the course out.

The Path Forward: Boring Consistency

I’m not going to promise a dramatic, 30-day shredded rebound. I’m not going to commit to some over-the-top transformation in a specific window to sell a program. The last thing I want to do is turn a real, difficult moment into a marketing campaign for a future version of myself that may or may not show up in the way I expect.

What I am going to do is the boring stuff. Move again, slowly, as the ankle rehab allows. Eat like an adult most of the time. Track my intake honestly, even when the numbers are high. Stop pretending the cruise, the boot, the elevator, and the 238 didn’t happen. They’re all part of the story, and the story is what gets me to wherever I go next. It’s about returning to the habits that defined me before the injury, one meal and one step at a time.

The Bigger Point

If you’ve ever taken a photo you almost deleted—a weigh-in, a reflection in a window, or a bad day captured in pixels—I’d encourage you to keep it. Not to punish yourself with it or to use it as a tool for self-loathing, but to remember. Memory is a strange thing; it tends to edit out the parts we don’t like. It smooths over the rough edges of our failures.

The photo doesn’t edit. The scale doesn’t edit. They’re blunt instruments, but they don’t lie. The number on the scale that day was 238 lbs. The number on the scale the next time I step on it will be whatever it is. The job isn’t to chase a specific number to find happiness. The job is to keep showing up between the weigh-ins, surrounded by people who make the journey easier, until the photo I take next looks a little different than this one. Until then, the photo stays as a reminder of where I started back.
