Research digest: Group memory can distort the evidence it preserves Compressing a language model group’s discussion into a short memory can make the final decision worse, even when the team had useful evidence. Two intuitive rules failed: keeping the rarest claims sometimes captured the useful facts, but keeping claims relevant to the decision criteria also pulled in plausible decoys because they were on topic. The deeper problem was social proof—the final model gave extra weight to facts attributed to many agents. Removing those attribution tags, or randomly reassigning them while keeping the discussion order fixed, improved accuracy. The research also found that a complete transcript could perform worse than taking no notes at all. This matters for any workflow that asks one model to distill another model group’s reasoning: a concise summary may preserve facts while distorting their importance. A concrete consequence is to evaluate memory systems on the balance of evidence they retain, rather than assuming that rarity or topical relevance is enough. Technical depth angle: The central mechanism is evidence balance. Rarity and relevance do not reliably identify which claims should survive into a final decision memory, especially when distractors stay on topic. The final model also treats attribution as social proof, giving claims more weight when many agents are associated with them. Accuracy improves when that attribution is removed or randomized, so preserving evidence is not enough; preserving its relative significance is also essential. Actionability angle: This means memory summaries for multi-agent decisions need to preserve which evidence is decisive, not merely which claims are rare or topically relevant. For a panel-to-one-model workflow, test the retained evidence mix and sensitivity to agent attribution before trusting the summary. Listener hook: If you use a model to compress expert or agent discussion, a shorter summary may not be a safer decision input.

[10:37] Research digest: EnvSched Targets More Useful Agent Training Runs

Training a language agent can become expensive quickly because each training run, or rollout, may involve different tools, states, transitions, and checks for success. EnvSched is a research scheduler designed to make a limited set of those runs more useful.

The paper’s central finding is that scheduling tasks together with their environments can improve the information in each training batch. EnvSched learns from rollout history, favors tasks likely to reveal meaningful reward differences, and spreads work across environments and interaction patterns that have received less attention.

To do that, it organizes task-environment pairs in a graph. Connections capture either relationships within an environment, such as shared tools and verification structures, or broader similarities in execution and rewards. Historical rollout information is used to estimate how much variation each task is likely to produce. Environment quotas and a similarity penalty then limit repetition.

The practical consequence is straightforward: with a fixed training budget, an agent team can concentrate runs where they are most likely to distinguish good behavior from bad, rather than spending too much of the budget on redundant experiences.

[08:38] Research digest: Group memory can distort the evidence it preserves

Nova A group of language models can discuss a problem at length and still lose the useful evidence when one model compresses that exchange into memory. Choosing the rarest claims sounds sensible, but the research found that it performs no better than random selection on average. Choosing claims that look relevant can be worse, because persuasive decoys are relevant too.

Alloy The stranger result involves social proof. A final decision maker gives more weight to a fact when the memory says many agents supported it. Removing those attributions—or randomly assigning them while preserving the discussion order—improves accuracy. Even the full transcript can perform worse than keeping no notes. So compression alone isn’t the villain. Evidence can be distorted by what gets retained, how distractors remain balanced against support, and which claims appear to carry a crowd behind them.

Nova That cuts directly into multi-agent systems that treat a summary as neutral shared memory. A concise record can erase disagreement or make repetition resemble independent confirmation. Preserving more text doesn’t automatically rescue the decision. The important distinction is between remembering that several agents said something and retaining the evidence that made the claim credible. Group memory has to carry substance, not merely consensus-shaped metadata.

Alloy Which is a useful check on agent swarms: adding voices can manufacture confidence faster than knowledge. If one summarizer decides what survives and another model trusts apparent agreement, the group can amplify a plausible mistake without changing the underlying evidence at all.

Alloy Agent training burns through expensive rollouts—complete attempts involving tools, changing states, and checks for success. If only a limited number can be run, how does EnvSched decide which experiences deserve the budget?
